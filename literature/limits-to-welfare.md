# Limits to Welfare

## Why animal welfare depends on demand reduction

Summary/TLDR
We cannot afford - biophysically, economically or practically - to give farmed animals decent lives at current numbers, let alone increased numbers as projected for the future
Limits to welfare mean welfare campaigning will hit ceilings when scaled up without concurrent reductions in demand
The projected growth in the Global South makes some of these limitations even more stringent
A reduction in demand - or prevention of increase in demand, in Global South context - is a necessary requirement to ensuring farmed animal welfare globally
EA animal welfare modelling should include ceilings to welfare to accurately predict cost-effectiveness
EA funders should balance welfare improvements and demand reduction to optimize long term impact, not prioritize between the two based on immediate SADs

# 1. The EA Status Quo: Welfare and Demand

Within EA farmed animal advocacy, interventions can broadly be divided into two main strategies:
Welfare improvements (improving the living conditions of farmed animals)
Demand reduction (reducing the total headcount of animals raised and slaughtered through e.g. alternative proteins, institutional dietary shifts, or policy work).
While both strategies have historically been important pillars of EA animal advocacy, EA grantmaking and advocacy have broadly prioritized welfare improvements, such as cage-free layer hen commitments and the European Chicken Commitment (ECC), over demand reduction. It is estimated that between 2019-2021, 60% of disclosed donations from EA animal welfare donors went to corporate animal welfare, with 9% going to alternate proteins (JamesOz, 2021). The EA Animal Welfare fund in its 2026-2028 priorities explicitly excludes “institutional meat reduction or alternative protein advocacy and development” (EAAWF, 2025).
This prioritization is supported by cost-effectiveness models, as well as by concerns over the tractability of demand-side work. Early evaluations of corporate welfare campaigns yielded astonishing results, estimating that they could affect 9 to 120 years of chicken life per dollar spent (Šimčikas, 2019). With most of the low hanging fruits reaped, Coefficient Giving estimates current cost-effectiveness to be about one fifth of Šimčikas’ original estimate (Oehlsen, 2024).
Demand reducing work is rated comparatively less. For instance, Grilo (2025) estimated an institutional demand reduction campaign (School Plates) to have 19.4% of the cost-effectiveness of cage-free corporate campaigns, and a consumer demand reduction campaign (Veganuary) only 1.20%. An anonymous contribution to the EA forum (BAB, 2025) models that a $100k donation to cage-free advocacy prevents ~550,000 hen-life-equivalents of suffering vs. ~166,000 for an alternative protein (precision fermentation) investment. In response, Bruers (2025) points at large uncertainties and recommends as a rule of thumb to treat cost-effectiveness estimates that show a difference smaller than an order of magnitude as not statistically significant.
Šimčikas (2025) compares retailer plant-based commitments to several welfare programmes and finds them all within an order of magnitude for animal years affected per dollar spent, with the plant-based commitments at the lower end. He notes that all estimates are highly speculative and that retailer plant-based commitments would be at least seven times higher if wild-caught fishes were included.
In recent years, demand reduction (particularly alternative protein development) has also started facing increasing skepticism due to slower technology rollouts (in particular, cultivated meat; Dullaghan, 2022), and uncertain consumer adoption (Peacock, 2023; Mazzarella, 2025). This has strengthened the argument for spending a marginal dollar on corporate welfare campaigns rather than attempting to reduce demand.
This strategic division rests on a fundamental flaw: it treats welfare improvements and demand reduction as independent, competing interventions. Standard EA models evaluate corporate campaign wins as if they can scale infinitely across the market (albeit with diminishing marginal returns), independent of baseline demand for animal products. In reality, welfare and demand are causally bound. As I hope to show in this article, welfare improvements will hit a ceiling (or rather, three distinct ceilings - a biophysical one, a socio-economic one and an animal welfare one) in the absence of concurrent reductions in total animal headcount.
There are, in short, limits to welfare improvements.

# 2. The Baseline Trajectory: Global Demand Growth

To understand why welfare improvements face structural limits, we must first establish the Business-as-Usual (BAU) trajectory of global animal agriculture over the coming decades.
According to baseline projections by the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) global meat production is projected to grow by 25% between now and 2050 (Our World in Data, 2026), driven both by population growth and rising per capita demand, particularly in the Global South.
Crucially, this growth is not evenly distributed across species. The vast majority of projected demand growth is concentrated in poultry and farmed fish, the species with the highest suffering per kilogram of meat produced.
Rethink Priorities projects that by 2033, the global number of broilers slaughtered will have increased 14,6% from 76.2B to 87.3B per year, and finfishes slaughtered will increase 16,1% from 133.6B to 155.1B per year (Moulange and Shah, 2025).
Global baseline demand growth is currently rapidly swamping local welfare gains [SOURCE]. Much of the growth (xx%) is expected to occur in the Global South. Whether welfare improvements will be able to scale to meet the growing demand will therefore depend in largest part on whether they effectively scale up in these regions. If not, the absolute number of animals suffering in intensive conventional systems globally will continue to rise - even if corporate welfare campaigns achieve 100% compliance across North America and Western Europe.
Now I will move to discuss the three main limits to welfare improvements: the biophysical limit, the socio-economic limit and the animal welfare limit.

# 3. The Biophysical Limit

The global food system is already pushing the Earth past its safe operating space. Under the Planetary Boundaries framework (Rockström et al., 2009; Steffen et al., 2015; Richardson et al., 2023), humanity has transgressed six of the nine boundaries necessary to sustain human civilization. Animal agriculture is a primary driver of these transgressions.
The below radar chart, taken from the 2025 EAT Lancet report, illustrates both the transgressions and, in dotted hatching, the food system contribution.

According to Poore & Nemecek (2018), livestock production occupies 83% of global agricultural land and generates 56%–58% of agricultural greenhouse gas emissions, despite supplying only 37% of human dietary protein and 18% of dietary calories. The current industrial baseline is already environmentally unsustainable.
Now this is a critique of industrial agriculture that most readers are probably well familiar with. The catch from an animal welfare point of view is that improvements in welfare tend to aggravate some of these impacts.
The oversized global impact of animal agriculture is mainly a product of the quantity of animals, combined with an inherent inefficiency of animal-based production. Conventional factory farming has worked hard to minimize this inefficiency, reducing the footprint per animal (or per unit of animal protein) by maximizing biological throughput. Broiler chickens are bred to reach slaughter weight in 35 days, while laying hens are housed in high-density cages to minimize heat loss and feed expenditure.
Improving animal welfare seems to inherently require dismantling these resource efficiencies. Providing animals with more living space, switching to slower-growing breeds, or putting animals on pasture lowers productivity per hectare and per animal. If animal headcount remains constant (or grows under business-as-usual scenarios), attempting to give animals better lives forces a dramatic expansion of the food system's biophysical footprint—accelerating the breach of planetary boundaries.
To quantify this:
For broilers: transitioning from current intensive practices to higher-welfare standards like the European Chicken Commitment (ECC) increases carbon emissions by 11%, land use by 14.4%, and phosphorus excretion by 36.7% per kg of meat (Wageningen Economic Research, 2025).
For layer hens: transitioning from an enriched cage system to a cage-free (barn) system increases carbon emissions per tonne of eggs by 56% and land use by 13% (Martinic et al., 2026).
These increases reflect marginal improvements in animal welfare that have been tailored to be realistic short-term asks. Systems that are more ambitious when it comes to animal welfare, such as organic farming, tend to have even higher land use and carbon emissions (Martinic et al., 2026)
All of this is not to say that welfare improvements should not be pursued or that environmental considerations should take precedence over the welfare of animals. What it does show is that we do not have the biophysical option space to give decent lives to all animals currently farmed, let alone the numbers of animals projected for the future. The only way we can afford to give all farmed animals decent lives (which it may be said we owe them) is if we consequently reduce their number.
A common counterargument is that the environmental cost of higher welfare is “worth it”, or that higher welfare systems (particularly organic farming), though they have increased impacts in some indicators (like carbon, nitrogen and phosphate), outperform caged systems in others (like biodiversity, pesticide and antibiotic use). This misrepresents the situation as a preferential trade-off rather than a matter of biophysical limits we do not have the luxury of transgressing.
The biophysical limit is likely to be particularly stringent in the Global South, where most of the growth of animal production is projected to be. Generally, these areas are already experiencing higher levels of resource and water scarcity compared to the Global North. Increasing welfare standards without addressing the main source - growing demand for animal protein - would exacerbate these problems
It may still be argued that other sectors could bear the brunt of maintaining total environmental impact within safe limits. Considering animal agriculture’s outsized role in the transgressions, this would be a tall order. It also does not change the fact that the biophysical limits to welfare translate into very concrete, practical limits.
From Biophysical Limits to Practical Operational Limits
While planetary boundaries operate at global or regional scales, they translate directly into concrete, operational bottlenecks that restrict the real-world execution of animal welfare campaigns.
Land Availability and Zoning Restrictions: Transitioning to lower stocking densities or free-range systems requires more physical acreage. In Western Europe and parts of North America, land scarcity, soaring real estate prices, and strict local zoning laws prevent producers from expanding farm footprints, making system conversions physically impossible for urban or land-constrained operations.
Feed Supply and Volatility: Higher feed conversion ratios leave high-welfare systems more exposed to global feed-grain market shocks. Surges in soy and grain prices directly raise the marginal cost of high-welfare meat, driving producers back toward hyper-efficient conventional strains.
Scope 3 Climate Commitments: Retailers and food corporations face mounting legal, investor and civil society pressure to reduce Scope 3 supply chain greenhouse gas emissions. Because high-welfare animal products carry higher carbon footprints per kilogram, corporate sustainability departments may clash with corporate animal welfare teams, delaying high-welfare commitments to protect corporate decarbonization targets.
Nitrogen and ammonia. In regions with dense animal agriculture (such as the Netherlands, Belgium, Denmark), local nitrogen and ammonia regulations severely restrict farm emissions. Regional environmental caps prevent farms from building the larger or more ventilated sheds required for higher welfare without downsizing their herds.
Abstract biophysical constraints quickly manifest as real-world legal, financial, and regulatory walls. Demand reduction is the physical prerequisite for high-welfare farming to be environmentally or practically viable.

# 4. The Socioeconomic Limit

Even if biophysical limits were temporarily ignored, welfare campaigns face an imminent economic ceiling. Welfare improvements do not follow a linear cost curve; as campaigns progress beyond the most basic welfare requirements, marginal production costs rise sharply.
A recent economic evaluations quantifies the financial friction of high-welfare broiler production: Wageningen Economic Research (2025) evaluated ECC implementation across six EU member states (Poland, Germany, Spain, France, Italy, and Netherlands). Transitioning to ECC broilers increases costs by between 19-20%, depending on the country. Cost spikes are primarily driven by capital expenditure on housing (+7 to +11 cents/kg) and higher feed costs (+6.8 cents/kg) due to lower food conversion rate. As with environmental impacts, cost increases will be significantly larger for more ambitious welfare improvements.
These costs increases will eventually have to be passed on to consumers. While in survey data, consumers overwhelmingly support farm animal welfare, in actual retail environments, price remains the primary driver of purchase decisions.
Economic studies consistently show that consumer Willingness-To-Pay (WTP) for animal welfare follows a steep decay curve:
A small minority (about 10-15%) of high-income consumers are willing to pay premiums of +30% or higher for organic or pasture-raised goods.
The mass market (about 70-80%) exhibits high price sensitivity. Beyond a 10% to 15% price increase, purchase intent drops sharply.
Without mandatory state regulation, voluntary high-welfare standards cannot capture mass-market share. Such regulation is occurring (e.g. Britain phasing out cage systems for layer hens) but is politically sensitive.
When welfare mandates cause noticeable spikes in grocery bills (e.g., egg prices rising 50%+), it triggers severe political pushback, accusations of regressive taxation on low-income families, and lobbying by agricultural groups. Governments may respond to food price spikes by rolling back mandates, granting compliance exemptions, or heavily subsidizing factory farming operations to keep retail prices artificially low.
Political and social pressure creates a hard ceiling on how far welfare mandates can push prices before the policy apparatus shuts them down.
Corporate Backsliding and Campaign Friction
This economic ceiling is already evident in the growing resistance met by corporate campaigns today.
When EA advocacy focused on cage-free egg pledges, the marginal cost per egg was negligible (1-2 cents), enabling high fulfillment rates (85-90%). However, as campaigns pushed into broiler welfare (the European Chicken Commitment / Better Chicken Commitment), asking food companies to absorb +20% to +40% production cost increases during a period of high inflation triggered widespread industry pushback:
Pledge Delays and Defaults: In the UK and Europe, major chains like KFC, Nando’s, Burger King, and Wagamama have delayed, scaled back, or dropped aspects of their ECC pledges, citing severe cost pressure and competitive disadvantage.
Progress Stagnation: According to Compassion in World Farming's ChickenTrack reports, over 58% of tracked European corporate commitments show zero public progress toward full Better Chicken Commitment compliance.
The takeaway: voluntary corporate welfare campaigns cannot scale across the remaining 80% of the market because they encounter a hard economic limit. Corporations will not voluntarily adopt reforms that destroy their operating margins unless regulatory mandates level the playing field or demand-side alternatives make high-meat diets obsolete.
Does not welfare reduce demand?
It might be argued that welfare reforms inherently solve the scaling problem by raising production costs, thereby raising consumer prices and driving down demand. However, this relies on a dangerous assumption. Because meat demand is inelastic (approx -0.4 to -0.7), if a comprehensive welfare reform increases retail meat prices by 20%, consumer demand drops by only 8% to 14%.
Current price increases of x/y% in Europe as a result of recent geopolitical events have also not led to a dramatic reduction in meat consumption. Meanwhile, animal consumption globally has continued to rise, with an emphasis on lower-cost sources such as broiler meat and eggs.
Because meat is inelastic, welfare price hikes only slow down the rate of growth of animal slaughter—they do not shrink absolute headcount. Relying on price elasticities alone is mathematically insufficient to counter global demographic shifts.
Price pressure alone cannot solve the scale problem and at worst reinforce small-bodied animal replacement. Demand-side interventions can act as a pressure valve by reducing cultural norms around animal consumption or normalizing alternatives that would enable true displacement.
Granted, the socioeconomic limit to welfare seems less ‘hard’ than the biophysical one. In affluent societies, a low willingness to pay for welfare may well be interpreted as a sign of moral degeneration or stinginess - not an immovable economic hurdle but something that decisive political action may solve and consumers will eventually adapt to. People in the past used to spend xx% of their income on food, and nowadays we spend yy% on electronics. Surely people can get used again to spending more on meat (or eating less of it).
Whether this argument is politically feasible or not, remains to be seen. It appears to be moving into a similar direction of political sensitivity that the debate on demand reduction is subject to. But what is definitely clear is that such arguments do not easily extrapolate to the Global South, where ZZ% of projected growth in animal production is expected to materialize.
Global South limit
The Global South is rapidly developing a taste for animal protein, fueled in part by industrial animal agriculture aggressively expanding their markets and offering cheap animal protein, and in other part by a need to tackle very real nutritional deficiencies. As said, most of the projected growth in farmed animal numbers is set to take place in the Global South.
The economic reality in these regions is such that a large part of consumers can hardly afford to pay more for higher welfare products. Nor can it be expected that farmed animal welfare is made a political priority when it conflicts with other policy goals of economic growth and nutrition. The low cost of in particular chicken sourced protein is an important part of its growing popularity.
Practical limitations to scaling up animal welfare programmes in the Global South are also becoming apparent, and are part of a larger mismatch between Northern strategies and Southern realities (Stray Dog Institute, 2026).
Differences in culture and market organization mean that the common strategy of targeting industry leaders (large caterers like Compass Group, or restaurant chains such as Nando’s) based on a credible threat to their public image, does not replicate easily in many Global South contexts. In markets that are less centrally organized, such central players either do not exist or are much less powerful. Infrastructure for verifying the sourcing of their products may also be less reliable or not in place.
Farmers in the Global South also generally have different priorities from their colleagues in the North and generally have less access to veterinary support. Infrastructure for standardizing and certifying production based on welfare standards may not be in place.
The challenge described above is not unique to animal welfare improvements and similar but different challenges will apply to demand-oriented work. I am certainly not suggesting that Global North-style approaches to demand can be introduced in a Global South context. Specific approaches will need to be developed that are suitable to the unique cultural, political and economic context of the country.
But when it comes to demand, the Global South has one important advantage that the Global North does not have. Many countries in the Global South are coming from a position where diets are already predominantly plant-based and food culture has not developed in the way it has in Europe, Australia and North America to sanctify the consumption of large quantities of animal products. In a multi-country survey commissioned by EAT Lancet (2025), people in Africa, Southeast Asia, and Latin America were much more likely to say they would adopt the Planetary Health Diet - a science-backed, predominantly plant-based diet - compared to people from Europe or North America.
This is perhaps not surprising as these countries’ current diets already do much more strongly reflect the Planetary Health Diet. Indeed there is a very strong correlation between consumer-stated willingness to adopt the Planetary Health Diet and the current share of plant protein in the national diet, as reported by Our World in Data (2023).

# 5. The animal welfare limit

The third and last limit I would like to propose is a feasibility limit to animal welfare itself. Though marginal improvements to animal welfare can certainly be made from the current situation, and at relatively low cost, there are clear limits to how far such improvement can be taken within the systemic boundaries that come with the production of very high numbers of animals. Even when monetary constraints are for the moment ignored, this system comes with other constraints, including spatial constraints and labor input.
These limits are harder to quantify compared to the socioeconomic and biophysical limit but no less real. They require us to ask what “good welfare” looks like. Or, another way of putting this: what does our end goal for farmed animals look like?
This question will be answered differently based on various worldviews. I will argue that in all or most worldviews that are prevalent within the animal welfare movement, achieving good welfare for animals is practically impossible without a reduction in total animals farmed.
The vegan / animal rights worldview: this position holds that the exploitation of animals in itself is unjustifiable because it intrinsically entails unnecessary harm to animals. The end goal is for no animals to be farmed. This worldview most clearly requires a reduction in animal headcount, to (close to) zero.
The animal welfare worldview: this position holds that exploitation of animals can be justifiable provided that certain standards are met with regards to their treatment and quality of life. These standards generally involve an absence of pain or prolonged suffering and freedom to express natural behaviors (e.g. the “Five Freedoms” of Brambell Commission, or similar frameworks). I will demonstrate that this view likely requires a reduction in demand by considering the following worldview, which has a lower bar.
The legal worldview: this position holds that animal welfare is provided when treatment and living conditions are in line with legal requirements. For illustration, we will consider current animal welfare regulations in the European Union, which are commonly regarded as being among the highest standards in the world. In as far as these are the result of a democratic process, they may be seen as a reflection of current European communis opinio on what the lives of farmed animals should look like - which almost by definition is lower than desired by animal welfare groups.
In addition to quantitative standards on e.g. minimum space requirements for animals, European regulations include more broad qualitative prescriptions. It would be expected, given the strong influence of animal agricultural lobby on this legislation, that these prescriptions are compatible with conventional husbandry practices. Even a very superficial inspection shows that they are not.
Comparison of EU legislation on broiler welfare and conventional reality
● Lack of Care & Failed Inspections
The law says: "It is prohibited for the keeper of an animal to withhold necessary care from an animal" (Article 37 of the Animal Health and Welfare Act). / "Any animal which appears to be ill or injured must be cared for appropriately without delay and, where such care does not bring about an improvement in its condition, a veterinarian must be consulted as soon as possible" (Article 4, paragraph 3 of the Decree on the Welfare of Production Animals). / "Where necessary, sick or injured animals shall be isolated in suitable accommodation with, where appropriate, dry comfortable bedding" (Article 5, paragraph 2 of the Decree on the Welfare of Production Animals) / "All chickens kept on the holding must be inspected at least twice a day. Special attention should be paid to signs indicating a reduced level of animal welfare and/or animal health" (Article 4 of the Broiler Decree, in conjunction with Annex I, point 8, of EU Directive 2007/43/EC).
The conventional reality:
The massive scale of commercial poultry operations makes mandatory daily individual inspections and care practically impossible. Consequently, signs of severely reduced welfare are systematically ignored. Sick and injured broilers are not isolated or given appropriate care—which would entail placing them on clean, dry litter—nor is a veterinarian structurally consulted.
As a result, millions of animals are left to suffer in their own feces without treatment. In the Netherlands, government-mandated research shows that a massive percentage of broilers suffer from severe, untreated injuries caused by bad litter quality: 38.4% suffer from severe footpad dermatitis (lesions), 20% suffer from hock burns, and 6.5% suffer from breast blisters. (De Jong et al., 2012 / De Jong, 2011b).
Harmful Climate
What the law says: "Air circulation, dust levels, temperature, relative air humidity and gas concentrations must be kept within limits which are not harmful to the animals" (Article 4 in conjunction with Annex, point 10 of EU Directive 98/58/EC).
The conventional condition:
The accumulation of feces on farms creates high concentrations of corrosive ammonia gas and dust. This harmful climate causes painful eye inflammations, skin burns, and severe airway damage, leading to an estimated 60-80% of chickens in the Netherlands suffering from respiratory and eye irritations.
Suffering during Slaughter
What the law says:
"Animals shall be spared any avoidable pain, distress or suffering during their killing and related operations" (Article 3, paragraph 1 of Regulation (EC) No 1099/2009).
"During movement, lairaging, restraint, stunning, slaughter or killing, animals shall be spared any avoidable excitement, pain, suffering, or avoidable injury or contusions" (Article 3 of the Decree on the Killing of Animals).
The conventional reality
During the slaughter process, roughly 50% of chickens are stunned using electrified water baths. Due to poor current distribution or chickens lifting their heads and missing the water, between 10% and 50% of these birds are not or insufficiently stunned. They experience severe, avoidable pain and stress, and risk being cut or submerged into scalding water while still fully conscious.
The above is adapted from an analysis by Dutch charity Dier & Recht, performed in 2014. Certain details may have changed since then but the overall picture is clear: conventional practice systematically deviates from what European law demands. The point is not that every individual farmer is maliciously ignoring legal requirements, but that the conventional system is not compatible with keeping them.
Pushing for even stricter regulations, or for more strict enforcement of current regulation, therefore does not seem very fruitful. Some of the discrepancies may very well be resolved without necessitating a reduction in total headcount, e.g. lower density of animals should already help reduce respiratory diseases. But others, such as the requirement that animals receive individual care and inspection, or ensure that they do not suffer during slaughter, seem very hard to reconcile with producing animals on a large scale.
If total headcount is to be kept stable (or growing) at lower density, this would mean either much larger farms or many more smaller farms, with a much higher involvement of staff. For slaughterhouses, the requirement that animals are spared avoidable pain would similarly require much more personnel, along with a much lower processing speed, to allow for animals to be handled individually and avoiding the insufficient stunning that occurs in automated processes.
This would not only increase cost, but also human labour requirement. In particular in Western countries, where agricultural employment has plummeted from 50-80% of the workforce a few centuries ago to under 5% today, and farm and slaughterhouse work increasingly depends on migrant workers, it is unclear that a sufficient and willing labor force exists to support a system of animal husbandry that abides by these standards. A reduction in total headcount would reduce pressure on the system to deliver such.
In the Global South, the employment picture could look different. But as desribed in the previous paragraph, economic considerations will provide a much more pressing ceiling to what welfare requirements are seen as feasible.
The productivity trade-off
The fact that higher welfare systems tend to be less productive introduces another trade-off in terms of animal welfare. Lower production in meat, eggs or milk per animal means that if productive output is to be maintained constant (or growing), total number of animals will have to increase.

# 8. Limitations and why I may be wrong

Future Affluence. A vastly wealthier global society may be able to afford expensive, high-welfare farming in the future, removing or at least pushing up the socio-economic ceiling.
Likelihood: hard to predict, but not in foreseeable future. If general affluence increases it is also far from guaranteed to be distributed equally.
Technological Decoupling. Future innovations in breeding or, more likely, feed (e.g., air-protein, precision fermentation for feed, genetic engineering) could ‘decouple’ welfare from environmental impact. It may even entirely remove land-use constraints, opening up vastly increased biophysical space for expanding animal production.
Likelihood: not immediately apparent, but possible. This could be a farm animal welfare s-risk and strengthen the argument for reduction. If we assume in this scenario that farmed animal lives remain net-negative on average, expanding housing capacity to hold far more "high-welfare" animals still multiplies net suffering.
AI farm work. The requirement of individual care for animals, for which I argue a labour force may not exist in the West, may in the future be provided with the help of artificial intelligence.
Likelihood: quite reasonable. Though this would reduce human labor, introducing such systems would still come at a significant monetary cost.
Limits to reduction. The obvious objection :) While there do not seem to be as strong biophysical or economic limits on scaling up alternative proteins, they still might fail on consumer acceptance and cultural norms. The aim of this article is not to present reduction as an ‘easier’ or higher-success rate strategy, but as a necessary complement. I intend to write a future post about promising opportunities in reduction.
